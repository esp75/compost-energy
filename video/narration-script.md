# Narration script: How compost makes heat

Timed to the captions in `compostito-explainer.mp4` (about 3 min 30 s). Read one line per caption window. A calm pace is about 150 words a minute, and the timings leave room for that. On-screen text that isn't narrated is listed in *italics*.

## Opening  (0:00.0 – 0:06.0)

*How compost makes heat · Farm waste in. Heat and fertiliser out.*

## 01 · What compost is  (0:06.0 – 0:26.0)

`00:08` Compost is what happens when microbes eat manure, straw, food and garden waste.

`00:13` Bacteria and fungi break down carbon-rich browns and nitrogen-rich greens, ideally mixed about 25–35 : 1.

`00:19` With oxygen, they turn it into CO₂, water, heat and humus, the dark, stable part of good soil.

## 02 · Aerobic vs anaerobic  (0:26.0 – 0:45.0)

`00:28` Microbes can work with oxygen (aerobic) or without it (anaerobic). The difference matters.

`00:32` Aerobic: fast, hot (55–70 °C) and almost odourless. Carbon leaves as CO₂ and most nitrogen stays in the compost.

`00:38` Anaerobic: slow and cool. A wet, airless pile gives off methane, about 28× as warming as CO₂, and loses nitrogen as ammonia.

## 03 · What the microbes need  (0:45.0 – 1:10.0)

`00:47` Microbes need four things: the right food mix, water, air, and a pile big enough to stay warm.

`00:52` Water: 50–60 %, as damp as a wrung-out sponge. Too dry and they stall. Too wet and water fills the air gaps, so the pile goes anaerobic.

`01:01` Air: at least 5 % oxygen in the gaps, so turn the pile or blow air through it. And at least about 1 m³, or it loses heat faster than it makes it.

## 04 · Microbes make heat  (1:10.0 – 1:29.0)

`01:12` As the microbes feed, they multiply, and their metabolism releases heat.

`01:16` Every kilogram of organic matter they break down releases about 18 MJ, roughly 5 kWh.

`01:21` The pile insulates itself, so heat builds up faster than it escapes: from 15 °C to over 60 °C in a few days.

## 05 · The four phases  (1:29.0 – 1:52.0)

`01:31` That rise and fall gives the four phases of composting.

`01:34` Mesophilic, 20–45 °C, for the first days. Then thermophilic, 45–70 °C: heat-loving microbes take over, and 3 days above 55 °C kill pathogens and weed seeds.

`01:43` Cooling and maturation: fungi and slower microbes finish the job over weeks to months. In an open pile, each turning brings oxygen back in.

## 06 · Where it started  (1:52.0 – 2:15.0)

`01:54` The project started with Compostito, a home composter with one chamber per phase and a water coil in the hot one.

`02:00` But compost passes heat on poorly: 99.5 % of the resistance is on the compost side, so a buried pipe collects only about 50 W.

`02:07` And a box that small loses its heat through the walls. At home the maths didn't add up, so the design moved to farm scale.

## 07 · The farm tunnel  (2:15.0 – 2:41.0)

`02:17` On a farm, the same process runs in four insulated tunnels. Each takes a 147 t batch of manure, silage and straw for 15 days.

`02:24` Fans blow air up through a slotted floor, and a controller holds the bed at 60 °C: about 4 days mesophilic, then 11 thermophilic.

`02:32` 72 hours above 55 °C sanitise every batch. It cools to 45 °C, then matures for 8 weeks in windrows, where no heat is collected.

## 08 · Heat and water  (2:41.0 – 3:08.0)

`02:43` The exhaust leaves the bed saturated at 60 °C. 92 % of its heat is in the water vapour, so the exchanger condenses it.

`02:50` That heats water to 50 °C for dairy hot water, barns and greenhouses: about 2 GWh a year from 14,279 t of feedstock.

`02:58` Taking that heat dries the bed: about 63 t of water leave each batch, and 44 t condense back. Spray bars rewet the bed if it falls below 52 %.

## 09 · A better fertiliser  (3:08.0 – 3:24.0)

`03:10` After 8 weeks of maturation, what comes out of the windrow is dark, crumbly compost.

`03:15` Because it was made with oxygen, it is stable and sanitised, holds its nutrients, and adds organic matter that keeps water and feeds soil life.
